Part A: Core Vocabulary and Real-World IT Application

1. Computer Ethics

Definition: Computer ethics is the study of what is right and wrong when people design, build, and use computers and information systems. It asks how technology affects people's rights, safety, and well-being, and guides IT professionals to make responsible choices even when the law is silent.

IT / Workplace Example: A system administrator has full access to every employee's email account. Reading a co-worker's private messages out of curiosity is technically easy, but it is unethical because the access was given only for maintenance, not for personal use.

2. Intellectual Property (IP)

Definition: Intellectual property is legal ownership over creations of the mind, such as software, designs, logos, inventions, and written content. In the Philippines, it is protected by Republic Act No. 8293 (Intellectual Property Code), where computer programs are covered by copyright and inventions can be patented.

IT / Workplace Example: A developer copies a GitHub project licensed under GPL into the company's closed-source product without following the license terms. This violates the original author's IP rights, even though the code was publicly visible.

3. Data Privacy and Informed Consent (Republic Act No. 10173, Data Privacy Act of 2012)

Definition: Data privacy is a person's right to control how their personal information is collected, used, stored, and shared. Informed consent means the person freely agrees to that processing after being clearly told what data is collected, why, and who receives it. Under RA 10173, organizations must follow transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality, and they are overseen by the National Privacy Commission (NPC).

IT / Workplace Example: An online enrollment system asks students for their address, birthday, and medical history. The school must show a clear privacy notice and get the student's agreement before collecting it, collect only what is needed, and report a data breach to the NPC if the database is hacked.

4. Cybercrime (Republic Act No. 10175, Cybercrime Prevention Act of 2012)

Definition: Cybercrime is any crime committed using or targeting computers, networks, or data. RA 10175 punishes offenses such as illegal access (hacking), illegal interception, data and system interference, computer-related fraud, identity theft, and cyber libel. Crimes under the Revised Penal Code done through ICT also receive a penalty one degree higher.

IT / Workplace Example: An employee sends fake "GCash verification" links to customers to steal their login details, then uses the accounts to transfer money. This counts as computer-related fraud and identity theft under RA 10175.

5. Algorithmic Bias

Definition: Algorithmic bias happens when a computer system produces results that are unfair to certain groups of people. It usually comes from biased or incomplete training data, poor design choices, or features that act as stand-ins for gender, race, or income.

IT / Workplace Example: A lending app's credit-scoring model gives lower scores to applicants from rural provinces because its training data mostly came from city borrowers. Qualified rural applicants are denied loans because of where they live, not because of their ability to pay.

6. Digital Divide

Definition: The digital divide is the gap between people who have reliable access to computers, the internet, and digital skills and those who do not. It can be caused by income, location, age, education, or disability.

IT / Workplace Example: During online classes in the pandemic, students in remote barangays without stable internet or a laptop fell behind classmates in the city. In the workplace, a government office that moves all its services to an online-only app excludes elderly citizens who have no smartphone.

7. Whistleblowing (in Tech/IT)

Definition: Whistleblowing is when an employee reports illegal, unsafe, or unethical practices inside their organization, first through internal channels and, if ignored, to regulators, authorities, or the public. In IT, it often involves hidden data misuse, security flaws, or harmful product decisions.

IT / Workplace Example: In 2021, former Facebook data scientist Frances Haugen released internal research showing the company knew its platforms could harm teen users, and she testified before the U.S. Congress. Locally, an IT staff member who reports to the NPC that their company is hiding a customer data breach is also whistleblowing.

8. Code of Ethics / Professional Conduct (ACM / IEEE / PSITE)

Definition: A code of ethics is a written set of principles that a professional organization expects its members to follow. Codes like the ACM Code of Ethics, the IEEE Code of Ethics, and the standards promoted by PSITE (Philippine Society of IT Educators) tell IT practitioners to avoid harm, be honest, respect privacy, honor IP, and put the public good first.

IT / Workplace Example: A project manager pressures a developer to skip security testing so the system can launch on time. Following the ACM Code's principle to "avoid harm," the developer documents the risk and refuses to sign off until critical vulnerabilities are fixed.

Part B: Quick Scenario Check

Scenario 1: The app that secretly collects and sells GPS locations

Terms that apply: Data Privacy and Informed Consent (primary), Whistleblowing, and Code of Ethics / Professional Conduct.

Reasoning: Location data is personal information, and collecting and selling it without disclosure violates the transparency and consent requirements of RA 10173, which the National Privacy Commission can penalize. The developer now faces a whistleblowing decision: under codes like the ACM Code, they should first raise the issue internally, and if the company refuses to fix it, they may report it to the NPC to protect users.

Scenario 2: Pirated software with a crack tool

Terms that apply: Intellectual Property (primary), Computer Ethics, and Cybercrime-related laws.

Reasoning: Using pirated software and bypassing its license key is copyright infringement under RA 8293, and it is worse here because the student earns money from it. Piracy done through computers is also penalized under the E-Commerce Act (RA 8792), and crack tools often carry malware that exposes client files. Ethically, the student is taking value from the software maker without paying, which no professional code allows.

Scenario 3: The resume-screening system that rejects qualified women

Terms that apply: Algorithmic Bias (primary), Code of Ethics / Professional Conduct, and Computer Ethics.

Reasoning: The system learned from 10 years of hiring data from a male-dominated department, so it treats being male as a sign of a "good" candidate and repeats past discrimination at scale. This goes against the ACM principle to "be fair and take action not to discriminate." The developers and HR team should audit the model, remove gender-linked features, retrain on balanced data, and keep human review in hiring decisions.
